Free VPS Hosting in 2026: What "Free" Actually Costs You

By Marcus Reid · Published Sep 23, 2026 · Last updated Sep 23, 2026 · 9 min read

Key Takeaways / The Verdict

  • Truly free, no-credit-card VPS hosting from a reputable provider does not exist. Every sustainable "free VPS" requires a payment card for identity verification.
  • Oracle Cloud Always Free is the only major offer with permanent, no-monthly-bill compute: 2× AMD E2.1.Micro (1 OCPU, 1 GB each) + Arm A1 capacity = 2 OCPUs / 12 GB RAM.
  • Google Cloud Free Tier gives one e2-micro (1 GB RAM, burstable vCPU, 30 GB disk) forever — but only in 3 US regions and with a 1 GB/month egress cap.
  • AWS Free Tier gives 750 hr/mo of a t2.micro or t3.micro (1 vCPU, 1 GB) for 12 months, plus a newer Free plan with up to $200 in credits over 6 months.
  • Best pick: Oracle for real free RAM, Google for the easiest US Linux box, AWS for a credit buffer to experiment. None are production-grade as your only server.

Everyone searching for free vps hosting wants the same thing: a virtual server they don't have to pay for. The honest answer is uncomfortable. There is no reputable company handing out a free vps server with no strings attached, because a server costs money to run and a business that gives one away forever is a business that closes. What does exist is a small set of always-free cloud tiers from the three largest infrastructure providers — and a long tail of sketchy "free VPS" offers that trade your data, your uptime, or your patience for the word "free." This guide compares the verified always-free options for vps hosting and explains exactly what "free" costs you in practice.

Detailed Breakdown

Oracle Cloud Always Free — the real free VPS

2× AMD E2.1.Micro (1 OCPU, 1 GB) + Arm A1 (2 OCPU / 12 GB) · 200 GB block storage · perpetual · card required

Oracle is the only hyperscaler that gives you compute with no expiry and no monthly bill. The AMD side is two small VMs — fine for a VPN, a personal DNS resolver, or a monitoring node. The interesting part is the Arm Ampere A1 allowance: 1,500 OCPU-hours and 9,000 GB-hours per month, which for an Always Free tenancy works out to 2 OCPUs and 12 GB of RAM that you can split into one or two instances. A single 2 OCPU / 12 GB Arm box is more useful than most paid $5 VPS plans.

The catch is capacity. Arm A1 capacity is frequently unavailable in the most popular regions, and signup itself can be fraught — Oracle's fraud checks reject some cards and some regions with no clear reason. The idle-reclamation rule (95th-percentile CPU, network, and memory all under 20% over 7 days) applies to A1 shapes, so you must keep something running. The AMD micro instances are exempt from reclamation. If you get in, it is the best free VPS hosting deal on the planet. If you can't get capacity, it's a dead end.

Pros

  • Truly perpetual, no monthly bill
  • Real RAM (up to 12 GB on Arm)
  • 200 GB boot storage included

Cons

  • Arm capacity often unavailable
  • Idle reclamation on A1 shapes
  • Signup friction / card rejections

Google Cloud Free Tier — the easy US Linux box

1× e2-micro (1 GB RAM, burstable vCPU) · 30 GB disk · 1 GB egress/mo · US-only · perpetual

Google's always-free tier is the simplest to use. One e2-micro instance — 1 GB of RAM with a burstable, shared vCPU — runs forever in Oregon, Iowa, or South Carolina, with 30 GB of persistent disk. There is no end date, and the $300 / 90-day Welcome credit (separate from the Free Tier) gives new customers a sandbox to try bigger machines. Setup is painless compared to Oracle.

The limits are the story. The 1 GB/month egress cap means almost any real visitor traffic incurs charges — a single modestly popular page can blow past it in a day. And the three-region lock means high latency for anyone outside North America. For a personal tunnel, a cron host, or a learning environment, it is perfect. For anything user-facing, the egress math pushes you to a paid plan fast.

Pros

  • Easiest signup of the three
  • No expiry on the e2-micro
  • $300 / 90-day credit for new users

Cons

  • 1 GB/month egress cap
  • US regions only
  • Burstable vCPU, shared

AWS Free Tier — the credit-and-micro combo

750 hr/mo t2.micro or t3.micro (1 vCPU, 1 GB) · 12 months · + up to $200 / 6 months (new Free plan)

AWS gives you 750 hours per month of a t2.micro or t3.micro — exactly one always-on 1 vCPU / 1 GB instance — for your first 12 months. On top of that, AWS now offers a Free plan for new customers with up to $200 in credits spread over 6 months, and the account auto-closes when the credits run out or the six months end, so you won't be surprised by a bill. EC2 is available on both plans.

The downside is the clock. After 12 months the micro tier stops being free, and the $200 plan expires in 6 months. AWS is the best choice if you want to learn the most in-demand cloud platform or need a credit buffer to spin up bigger temporary machines. It is the worst choice if you want something that stays free indefinitely — that is Oracle's turf.

Pros

  • Most in-demand cloud skills to learn
  • Up to $200 / 6-month credit buffer
  • Account auto-closes — no surprise bills

Cons

  • Micro tier expires after 12 months
  • $200 plan ends in 6 months
  • 1 vCPU / 1 GB is tight

The Gap Most "Free VPS" Articles Miss

The typical "best free VPS" roundup is a list of providers with affiliate links and a sentence each. It almost never tells you the three things that actually determine whether a free VPS is usable: (1) whether it requires a card (it does, for anything legitimate), (2) the egress or region limits that quietly turn "free" into "paid" the moment you get traffic, and (3) the idle-reclamation and capacity risks that mean your "free server" may not exist when you need it. This article leads with those constraints because they are the difference between a free VPS that teaches you something and one that wastes your afternoon.

We also deliberately exclude vendor-owned promotional pages and app-store listings from our comparison set — they tell you who occupies the search head, not what content gap exists. Who Should Pick Which Free VPS

You want a real, permanent free server with usable RAM

Pick Oracle Cloud Always Free, specifically the Arm A1 allocation (2 OCPU / 12 GB). Be prepared to retry signup and hunt for an availability domain with capacity. Keep a lightweight workload running so the idle rule doesn't reclaim it. This is the only option that stays free forever with meaningful specs.

You want the fastest path to a free Linux box in the US

Pick Google Cloud's e2-micro. Signup is smooth, the instance is ready in minutes, and there is no expiry. Accept the 1 GB/month egress cap and US-only regions as the price of that simplicity. Ideal for a personal tunnel, a cron host, or learning Linux.

You want to learn AWS or need a credit buffer to experiment

Pick AWS — the 12-month micro tier plus the up to $200 / 6-month Free plan credits. Use the credits to try bigger temporary machines you could never afford on a paid plan. Just set a calendar reminder: it ends, and you'll need to either upgrade or migrate.

You want zero card and total anonymity

There is no reputable answer. A "free VPS server" with no card is, at best, a short trial that wants your social shares or referrals, and at worst a box on oversold hardware run by an operator who may vanish. Do not put anything you care about on it. If anonymity matters, pay $3–6/month for a cheap VPS instead — it is the honest price of a server you control.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there really a free VPS hosting plan with no credit card?

Not from a reputable, sustainable provider. The genuinely free options that last — Oracle Cloud Always Free, Google Cloud Free Tier, and AWS Free Tier — all require a valid payment card for identity verification. Providers that advertise a free VPS server with no card almost always impose heavy limits, require referrals or social sharing, place your instance on oversold hardware, or disappear within months. Treat a no-card "free VPS" offer as a trial or a risk, not infrastructure you can depend on.

What is the best truly free VPS in 2026?

Oracle Cloud Always Free is the only major offering that gives you real, permanent, no-monthly-bill compute: up to two AMD E2.1.Micro instances (1 OCPU and 1 GB RAM each) plus Arm Ampere A1 capacity equivalent to 2 OCPUs and 12 GB of RAM. Google Cloud's e2-micro (1 GB RAM, burstable vCPU, 30 GB disk) is the easiest to spin up but is locked to three US regions and has a 1 GB/month egress cap. AWS gives 750 hours per month of a t2.micro or t3.micro (1 vCPU, 1 GB) for 12 months, plus a newer Free plan with up to $200 in credits over 6 months.

Why does Oracle reclaim idle Always Free instances?

Oracle's Always Free Arm A1 instances can be reclaimed if, over a rolling 7-day window, their 95th-percentile CPU utilization, network utilization, and memory utilization all stay below 20%. The policy exists to stop people from reserving capacity they never use. Keep a small, legitimate workload running (a personal VPN, a low-traffic site, a monitoring agent) and the instance stays yours. The AMD E2.1.Micro instances are not subject to this idle-reclamation rule.

Is a free VPS hosting tier good enough for production?

For a personal project, learning environment, or proof-of-concept, yes. For production traffic, no — not as your only server. The catches matter: Google caps free egress at 1 GB per month (any real traffic blows past it), AWS and Azure micro tiers expire after 12 months, Oracle capacity for Arm instances is frequently unavailable in popular regions, and every free tier is shared hardware with no SLA. Use free VPS hosting to learn and prototype; move to a paid plan (often under $6/month) before money or uptime depends on it.

Bottom Line

Free VPS hosting is real, but it is not free of conditions. The three sustainable options — Oracle Always Free, Google Cloud Free Tier, and AWS Free Tier — all want a card, all impose limits, and none should be your only production server. Oracle wins on permanent, usable capacity if you can get in. Google wins on simplicity and US availability. AWS wins on learning value and a credit buffer.

If your search for a free vps server is really about avoiding a $5/month bill, reconsider: a cheap paid VPS gives you predictable egress, a real SLA, and no idle-reclamation anxiety. Free is a great place to learn and prototype. Paid is where you build.
