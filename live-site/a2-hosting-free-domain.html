Is the domain really free, and what is it actually waiving?

The free domain is a waived registration fee, not a domain you own for free forever. hosting.com lists Free Domain Name Registration among the inclusions on its Shared Web Hosting lineup, and the same company sells domains as a separate product with prices labelled “for the 1st year” — .com at $11.99 — plus its own promise that “You always see the renewal price before you buy, not just a cheap first year.” So the plan covers the first registration; the domain renews on a second line, on a second clock. Whether the 30-day money-back guarantee reaches the domain fee is stated nowhere I could read — that one is Unconfirmed.

What the line item is actually waiving

On hosting.com’s hosting page, reviewed 2026-10-27, “Free Domain Name Registration” sits inside a list of Starter-plan inclusions, between 15GB NVMe disk space and malware detection. The full list as published there: 5 websites, AI Website Builder for WordPress, emails & security, 15GB NVMe disk space, free domain name registration, malware detection & removal, free managed migration, free SSL certificate, daily backups, and an OpenAPI and MCP server.

Read that list carefully and you notice what kind of item the domain is. It is grouped with SSL certificates, backups and migrations — things the provider performs for you at no extra charge. A free SSL certificate is a certificate you did not pay for. A free migration is labour you did not pay for. By the same grammar, a free domain name registration is a registration charge you did not pay for.

That distinction is the whole question. A waived fee is a discount on a purchase. It is not a statement about what the thing costs afterwards, and it is not a statement about ownership. The hosting page says nothing about the domain’s renewal price, nothing about whose name appears as registrant, and nothing about what happens to the domain if the hosting plan ends. Those are not omissions in the marketing copy — they belong to a different page, because the domain is a different product.

So the structure is identical to the one people already know from hosting discounts: a low first-year number, then a regular rate. The free domain does not escape that structure. It moves the first-year charge off your cart and into the plan’s inclusion list, and leaves the renewal exactly where it was — on the domain.

Two clocks, and they do not line up

Hosting and domains are billed on different rhythms, and that is where the surprise usually comes from.

The hosting rhythm is the prepaid term. hosting.com’s renewal-pricing page puts it this way: “We offer incredible savings when paying for a year or two or three in advance. If you choose monthly, or after that prepaid period ends, your website plans will renew at our regular pricing.”

The domain rhythm is per year, priced as “1st year” on the domain page. If you take a three-year hosting term to get the better rate, the domain does not automatically inherit that three-year horizon. Whether hosting.com lets you prepay the bundled domain for the full hosting term is not stated on either page — Unconfirmed.

The practical consequence is worth stating plainly: on a 36-month hosting term, a domain that renews annually produces a charge in month 13 that has nothing to do with the hosting renewal you thought you had already handled. Two dates, one invoice. Put both in a calendar on the day you buy, not on the day you notice.

The 30-day guarantee, and the question it does not answer

hosting.com states a 30-day money-back guarantee on its hosting plans, alongside a 99.9% uptime SLA, and separately advertises “no lock-ins or minimum terms.” Leaving is allowed. That part is clear.

What is not clear is what happens to the domain when you do. Neither the hosting page nor the domain page, as reviewed 2026-10-27, says whether a refunded hosting order also refunds the bundled domain registration, or whether the registration is kept and charged. That matters more than it sounds, because a domain is not a service you stop consuming — a registration is a commitment the registrar has already made upstream, and domain refunds are commonly restricted rather than automatic.

I am not going to guess at hosting.com’s answer here. Ask support in writing before you rely on it, and keep the reply. This is the single highest-value question to ask at checkout, because it is the only one where being wrong costs you the domain rather than the discount.

“No hidden costs” and the one exception on the same page

The domain page opens its inclusions section with “Everything a domain here comes with — No hidden costs, no fine print.” Six items follow. One of them reads: “WHOIS & ID privacy — Keep your name, address and email out of the public record and cut down the spam. Free on eligible extensions, charged on others.”

That is the exception, stated honestly and in the same breath. It is not hidden — but it is also not resolved: “eligible” and “others” are not defined on the page, so the cost of privacy depends on the extension you choose, and you only find out which side you are on once you have picked one. If privacy is the reason you are registering, check that line before you commit to a TLD, not after.

The other inclusions on that list are worth knowing because they are the ones coupon pages never mention: free DNS management, domain theft protection described as “a registrar lock that stops your domain being moved out of your account without your say-so,” custom nameservers and subdomains, and the transfer mechanic — “Most transfers add a year to your registration. You will need the EPP code from your current registrar.” That last line is the one genuinely different deal in the whole family: a transfer buys a year of registration life rather than discounting a year you were going to buy anyway.

Where DealDrop, HotDeals and TrulyCodes fall short

All three run a single store page for A2 Hosting that lists headline percentage discounts and a rotating set of codes. None of them splits the offer into its two lines.

“Free domain” appears on those pages as a bullet in a features list. It does not appear with the renewal price for the extension, with the registrant details, with the privacy exception, or with what happens to the domain on cancellation. That is not laziness so much as unit mismatch: their unit of work is a code, and the question a reader actually has is about a line item on an invoice. A page built around codes has nowhere to put a renewal date.

The gap is narrow and specific, which is what makes it worth filling: the four things that decide whether the free domain is worth anything — renewal price, registrant, cancellation behaviour, privacy eligibility — are each one sentence away from being answerable, and none of the three pages spends that sentence.

Five checks before you rely on the free domain

  1. Price the renewal, not the first year. Open hosting.com’s domain pricing for your exact extension and write down the renewal figure next to the first-year figure. The difference is the real cost of the “free” domain.
  2. Decide which cart the domain belongs in. Registered on the hosting order, it is an inclusion; bought separately, you see its renewal at the moment of purchase. Both are legitimate — but only one of them shows you the second number without asking.
  3. Ask, in writing, whether the 30-day guarantee covers the domain fee. Keep the answer. This is the only question where a wrong assumption costs you the name rather than the discount.
  4. Check privacy eligibility for your extension. “Free on eligible extensions, charged on others” is a real cost on the ones that are not eligible.
  5. Record two dates. Hosting term end, and domain renewal. They are not the same date, and the second one arrives first more often than people expect.

Frequently asked questions

Is the A2 Hosting free domain free forever?

No. hosting.com lists Free Domain Name Registration as an inclusion on its Shared Web Hosting lineup, which waives the registration charge on the order. The same company sells domains as a separate product, and every price on that product page is labelled for the 1st year — so the domain carries its own renewal price on its own schedule.

Whose name is the domain registered in?

hosting.com's hosting page and domain page, as reviewed on 2026-10-27, do not state the registrant details of a domain bundled with a plan. Unconfirmed — check the registrant contact on the order confirmation, or ask support before you buy.

What happens to the domain if I cancel the hosting plan?

Not stated on either page as reviewed. Unconfirmed. The domain page does mention Domain theft protection — “a registrar lock that stops your domain being moved out of your account without your say-so” — but that is a lock against unauthorised transfers, not a statement about what happens when you cancel hosting.

Is WHOIS privacy included with the free domain?

Partly. hosting.com's domain page says WHOIS & ID privacy is “Free on eligible extensions, charged on others”. Which extensions are eligible is not listed on that page, so the answer depends on the extension you pick.

Does transferring a domain in get me a free year?

hosting.com states: “Most transfers add a year to your registration. You will need the EPP code from your current registrar.” That is different from a discount — the transfer buys a year of registration life rather than reducing the price of a year you were going to buy anyway.
