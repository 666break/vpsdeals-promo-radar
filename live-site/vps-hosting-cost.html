VPS Hosting Cost and Pricing: What $3.88 to $96 a Month Actually Buys

By Marcus Reid · Published Oct 28, 2026 · Last updated Oct 31, 2026 · 9 min read

What a VPS actually costs, and what the sticker leaves out

$3.88 to $96.00 a month is the whole range in the plans we could read on 2026-10-30 — Namecheap's Spark at $3.88 on an annual term at the bottom, DigitalOcean's 16 GiB / 8 vCPU Droplet at $96.00 at the top. At the one shape every provider sells, 4 GB of RAM and 2 vCPU, the same server costs $4.54 at OVHcloud and $24.00 at DigitalOcean — 5.29× for identical specs, and $42.00 at DigitalOcean if you insist on dedicated cores instead of shared ones.

Where the money leaks — one line each, every figure from the provider's own page:

  • Add-ons. The plan price is the server alone: DigitalOcean bills block storage from $10/month and load balancers as separate products on the same pricing page. (digitalocean.com/pricing, read 2026-10-28)
  • Backups. DigitalOcean charges 20% of the Droplet price for weekly backups and 30% for daily — $4.80/month on the $24.00 Droplet. (docs.digitalocean.com/products/backups/details/pricing, read 2026-10-31)
  • Snapshots. Billed as storage, not as a feature: $0.06 per GiB-month with a $0.01 minimum at DigitalOcean, which is $4.80/month for a full disk on the $24.00 plan. (read 2026-10-31)
  • Bandwidth overrun. Cannot be computed — none of the three publishes an overage rate: DigitalOcean's table stops at 1,000 GiB outbound, OVHcloud says unlimited, ScalaHosting says unmetered. (read 2026-10-31)
  • A second IPv4. $0.60/month at Hetzner, $2.40 at OVHcloud, $5.00 at DigitalOcean — and powering the server off stops none of them. (read 2026-10-28)
  • Renewal. There is no first-term-then-renewal jump to model on VPS: OVHcloud's yearly sticker is exactly 12 × its monthly ($54.48 vs $4.54). The 4.7× renewal trap on this site is shared hosting, not VPS. (read 2026-10-28)

The verdict: a 4 GB / 2 vCPU box that is actually usable — with backups and a second IP — is $8.34/month at OVHcloud ($4.54 + $1.40 premium backup + $2.40 IPv4) and $33.80/month at DigitalOcean ($24.00 + $4.80 weekly backup + $5.00 IPv4): 4.05× for the same shape, before tax. Budget the middle of that range, not the sticker.

Searching for vps hosting cost usually gets you one number: the “from” price on the provider's homepage. That number is real, but it is the smallest plan in the range, with the add-ons stripped out and the tax line removed. This page takes the three official pricing pages we could actually open and read on 2026-10-28 — OVHcloud, DigitalOcean and Hetzner — and shows what each one states, what it leaves out, and which line items decide what you will really pay. Every figure below is copied from the provider's own page; where a page did not state something, we say so instead of filling the gap.

Which is why ranking VPS prices by RAM alone goes wrong, and why “the price of a VPS” has no single answer: a 2 GiB plan can be $12.00 or $18.00 at the same provider on the same day, and the difference is not margin — it is a second core.

Why the Same Provider Charges €6.49 and €43.49

This is the single most useful fact in VPS pricing, and it is sitting on one page. Hetzner splits its cloud range into three tiers by what the CPU is, not by how much RAM you get: Cost-Optimized at €5.99/month, Regular Performance at €6.49/month, and General Purpose with “Dedicated vCPUs” at €43.49/month, all excluding VAT. The top tier is 6.70× the second and 7.26× the entry tier, and the page's own description explains why: the cheap tiers share physical cores (“inexpensive due to shared resources”), the expensive tier does not.

DigitalOcean sells the same contrast inside one product line, which is cleaner to read because RAM, core count and provider are all held fixed:

That is a 1.75× premium for CPU isolation alone, with RAM, core count and provider held fixed. The page describes CPU-Optimized as “fast (2.6GHz+), dedicated vCPUs” and Basic as suited to “bursty applications that can handle variable levels of CPU” — the provider saying in its own words that the cheap tier shares cores. Put OVHcloud's VPS-1 beside that second row and the same 4 GB / 2 core shape spans 9.3×, $4.54 to $42.00, without either price being wrong.

The multiplier you actually pay for isolation therefore sits between 1.75× at DigitalOcean and 7.26× at Hetzner — a four-fold disagreement between two providers on what the same guarantee is worth. Both ends are the providers' own published prices, read on 2026-10-31.

That distinction is what actually separates a $4 VPS from a $40 VPS in every provider's range, and it is invisible if you only compare RAM. A shared-core box is fine for a blog, a staging environment, a small API and anything that idles most of the day. It is the wrong purchase for a workload that needs predictable latency — a database under steady write load, real-time bidding, CI runners, game servers — because when a neighbour on the same physical host spikes, your CPU time gets queued behind theirs. You are not buying 2 vCores; you are buying a share of 2 vCores.

So the first question to answer before you compare prices is not “how much RAM do I need” but “can I tolerate noisy neighbours”. If the answer is yes, the entry tier is genuinely good value. If it is no, you are shopping in a different part of the range and the $4 numbers are irrelevant to you.

What the Entry Price Leaves Out

Three things, consistently.

1. Backups. OVHcloud is unusually generous here and it is worth saying so: the VPS price includes “daily backup of the previous 24 hours”. That covers the most common disaster — you broke something yesterday — but not the one that hurts most, which is discovering a corrupted database three weeks later. For that, the page lists a premium automatic backup with a rolling 7-day restore from $1.40/month, and snapshots from $0.40/month. On a $4.54 plan, the 7-day backup is a 31% increase on the sticker. Most “VPS cost” comparisons never mention it.

2. Storage and network services. DigitalOcean's pricing page lists these separately from the Droplet: block storage (Volumes) from $10/month, load balancers from $12/month, managed databases from $15/month, and Kubernetes from $12/month. A $4 Droplet running a database that you actually care about usually becomes a $4 Droplet plus a $15 managed database, because that is the difference between “I have a server” and “I have a service”. None of that appears in the headline.

3. Tax. Hetzner states its prices “per month, excl. VAT”. If you are comparing a €5.99 Hetzner plan against a $4.54 OVHcloud plan, you are comparing a pre-tax euro figure against a dollar figure, and depending on where you are billed the gap can close or widen. There is no honest way to rank two providers without putting both on the same tax and currency basis first.

The Billing Clock Is Part of the Cost

DigitalOcean's page states that Droplets are billed “per-sec billing with 60 sec minimum or $0.01, whichever is higher”. That is a real feature and it changes the economics of certain workloads completely: if your pattern is spin up a 16 GB box, run a build for twenty minutes, destroy it, you pay for twenty minutes. On a provider that bills monthly, that same workflow costs a full month.

The flip side is the floor. A 60-second minimum means a server you create and delete in five seconds is not free, and if you automate aggressively — CI that provisions a box per commit — the minimum charge becomes the dominant term, not the per-second rate. Neither model is better in the abstract; the question is whether your usage is spiky or flat. Flat usage rewards monthly pricing and annual prepay. Spiky usage rewards per-second billing.

The monthly price itself is a cap, and it is not the 730 hours you get from 365 × 24 ÷ 12. DigitalOcean prints an hourly rate and a monthly price for every Droplet row, so the two can be put side by side and the meeting point found:

  • Never quote “hourly × 730” as the monthly price. It overstates every row in the table by 8.59–8.65%. The published monthly figure is already the cheaper of the two.

How to Price a VPS in Five Minutes

Work through these in order and the sticker price stops mattering:

  1. Decide shared or dedicated CPU. If the workload is latency-sensitive, only look at dedicated-vCPU tiers — the cheap rows are not candidates.
  2. Write down the add-ons you need. Backup retention beyond 24 hours, snapshots, block storage, a load balancer, a managed database. Get each one's stated price from the provider's own page.
  3. Put both providers on the same basis. Same currency, same tax treatment, same term. A pre-tax euro figure is not comparable to a dollar figure.
  4. Multiply by 12, not by 1. A $4.54 plan is $54.48 a year; the same shape at $24.00 is $288.00. Entry prices are often promotional or first-term, and the yearly number is what you will actually feel.
  5. Check the billing granularity if your usage is spiky, and the minimum charge if you automate provisioning.

What Most “VPS Hosting Cost” Pages Leave Out

We could not complete a proper competitor comparison for this page: the benchmark list of three third-party sites is still pending from the site owner, so the following is a structural observation from the three official pricing pages we read, not a survey of rival articles.

On those three pages, the “from” price is always the smallest plan, the shared-CPU tier, without backups, without extra storage, and — at Hetzner — without VAT. Every one of those four things is disclosed on the page, usually within a few hundred pixels of the headline number. The information is not hidden; it is just not aggregated. A cost page that stops at “from $4” has repeated the marketing line and skipped the four facts that decide the bill.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does VPS hosting cost per month?

The lowest verified entry prices as of 2026-10-28 are DigitalOcean Droplets from $4/month, OVHcloud VPS-1 from $4.54/month (2 vCores, 4 GB RAM, 40 GB SSD NVMe, unlimited traffic) and Hetzner's Cost-Optimized cloud plan from €5.99/month excluding VAT. Those are real prices, but each one is the smallest plan in the range and excludes anything sold as an add-on.

Why does the same provider list one plan at €6.49 and another at €43.49?

Because the cheap plan shares physical CPU cores and the expensive one gives you dedicated vCPUs. Hetzner's own cloud page shows Cost-Optimized at €5.99/month, Regular Performance at €6.49/month and General Purpose (dedicated vCPUs) at €43.49/month, all excluding VAT. That is roughly a 6.7x gap inside one provider, driven by CPU isolation rather than RAM.

What is included in a VPS price and what costs extra?

It varies by provider, which is exactly why the headline number misleads. On OVHcloud's VPS range the price includes a daily backup of the previous 24 hours and unlimited traffic, while a premium automatic backup with a rolling 7-day restore is listed from $1.40/month and snapshots from $0.40/month. At DigitalOcean, block storage (Volumes) is listed from $10/month, load balancers from $12/month and managed databases from $15/month, all separate from the Droplet.

Does VPS hosting bill monthly or per second?

Both exist. DigitalOcean states per-second billing with a 60-second minimum or $0.01, whichever is higher, so a server you delete after four minutes still costs the minimum. OVHcloud and Hetzner advertise monthly prices on their pricing pages. If you spin instances up and down for testing, the billing granularity matters more than the sticker price.

Are the advertised VPS prices before or after tax?

It depends on the provider and your location. Hetzner's cloud page states its prices per month excluding VAT, so the amount you pay depends on the VAT rate applied to your country. The US-dollar figures on OVHcloud's and DigitalOcean's pages are quoted as dollars, and euro and dollar prices are not interchangeable. Always check the currency and the tax line before comparing two providers.

Bottom Line

VPS hosting genuinely starts at about $4 to $6 a month, and for a small site on a shared-core plan that is a fair price, not a bait price. What the headline cannot tell you is which of the four cost drivers applies to you: shared versus dedicated CPU (a 6.7x gap at one provider), backup retention (a 31% add-on on a $4.54 plan), separately-billed storage and network services, and tax and currency. Answer those four and the monthly number falls out — usually somewhere between $55 and $520 a year, for the same three words on the invoice.
